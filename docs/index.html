How to read this

  • Each dot is one job. A job posted once per city or country counts once. Only jobs still listed on the last collection are shown.
  • Per hour, or per task or project: one at a time. The switch at the top picks which jobs are shown, because an hourly rate and a fee per task or project do not belong on the same scale. Jobs that give no pay figure have their own view, in the table.
  • The pay axis is the top of the stated range, in US dollars. "$40–$75 an hour" counts as $75, and "up to $75" counts as $75 too, so jobs that only give an "up to" figure probably pay less than shown.
  • Salaries are turned into an hourly rate at 40 hours a week (a year is 2,080 hours), only when the listing gives no hourly rate. Salaried jobs usually come with benefits that a contractor rate does not, so they are not a like-for-like comparison.
  • Other currencies are converted to US dollars at the European Central Bank's rate of the day the data was prepared (see the bottom of the page). "$" with no currency is taken as US dollars: the "Currency" grouping shows how sure that is.
  • Boxes show the middle half of the jobs in a group; the thick line is the median (half the jobs pay more, half less). The whiskers reach the furthest jobs that are not unusually far out. Groups with fewer than 5 jobs get dots only.
  • The log axis gives each doubling the same space, so $10 vs $20 looks as far apart as $100 vs $200. Switch to "Linear" to see plain distances.
  • Read from the listings by rules, not by hand. The field is guessed from the job title. Degrees are the ones the listing mentions, not always required. Click a row in the table to see the sentence each value came from.
  • Comparisons, not causes. A group paying more does not mean that trait causes higher pay: language jobs, for example, both ask for fewer degrees and pay less.
